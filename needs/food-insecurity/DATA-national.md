# National data — food insecurity (United States)

> Evidence for [NEED.md](NEED.md) §2, §4, and §6. Gathered 2026-09-12.
> Every figure is quoted from the source named beside it and was checked against
> that source on the date above. Figures are **national**: they say the need is
> large and widespread; they do not say what the gap is in any one community.

---

## 1. How many people

| Figure | Value | Year of data | Source | Kind |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Households food insecure at some time in the year | **13.7%** | 2024 | USDA ERS, *Household Food Security in the United States in 2024* (ERR-358) | stated (household survey) |
| Households with **very low** food security | **5.4%** | 2024 | USDA ERS, ERR-358 | stated |
| People living in food-insecure households | **47.9 million** | 2024 | USDA ERS data, as reported by FRAC | stated |
| Children living in food-insecure households | **14.1 million** | 2024 | USDA ERS data, as reported by FRAC | stated |
| Prior year, for comparison | 13.5% of households | 2023 | USDA ERS, as reported by the Alliance to End Hunger | stated |
| Food insecurity rate (people) | **14.4%**, ~48 million people incl. 14 million children | 2024 | Feeding America, *Map the Meal Gap 2026* | inferred (modelled) |

**Why "stated", not "revealed":** the USDA measure is a survey. Households answer
questions about running out of food and skipping meals. It is the most rigorous
national measure there is, but under the rules of evidence it is still what
people *said*. It establishes that the need exists at scale; it cannot identify
a local gap.

## 2. Who is most affected

Food-insecure household rates, 2024, USDA ERS data as reported by FRAC:

| Group | Rate |
|---|---|
| Single-mother households | 36.8% |
| Black households | 24.4% |
| Hispanic households | 20.2% |
| White, non-Hispanic households | 10.1% |
| Southern region | 15.0% |
| Urban areas | 16.0% |
| Rural areas | 15.9% |
| Suburban areas | 11.9% |

From *Map the Meal Gap 2026* (modelled, 2024 data):

- More than 12 million seniors and older adults were food insecure: 7.7 million
  aged 60+ (9.5%) and 4.9 million aged 50–59 (12.4%).
- More than 8 out of 10 counties with the highest estimated rates are rural or
  in the South.
- Child food insecurity reaches an estimated 45% in the worst-affected county.

**For pilot selection:** urban and rural rates are nearly identical and both well
above suburban. That supports choosing pilots that differ by setting (PROBE §2).
A rural pilot in the South would sit where the modelled rates are highest.

## 3. The gap between need and help — the most important figure here

> "approximately **25 million people, nearly half** of all people experiencing
> food insecurity, had incomes too high to qualify for federal nutrition
> assistance" — Feeding America, *Map the Meal Gap 2026* (modelled, 2024 data)

- The national food budget shortfall "surpassed $33 billion".
- People in food-insecure households needed an additional **$23.16 per person,
  per week**.

**Why it matters for the brief:** if the model is right, roughly half of
food-insecure people cannot get federal help *by design*, so charitable food and
community programs are their only formal support. That makes "households just
above benefit eligibility" a leading candidate gap. It remains a **hypothesis to
listen for, unprompted**, not a conclusion: it is modelled, national, and says
nothing about any specific community.

## 4. Why now

### SNAP changes under H.R. 1 (P.L. 119-21)

As summarized by the National Association of Counties (page dated 2025-10-31):

| Change | Detail | Effective |
|---|---|---|
| Work requirements widened | Now apply to adults aged **18 to 64** (previously 18 to 54), and to those with dependents **aged 14 and up** (previously excluded) | On enactment (2025) |
| Exemptions removed | Veterans, people experiencing homelessness, and former foster youth | On enactment |
| Waivers narrowed | Only areas with unemployment above **10%** | On enactment |
| Noncitizen eligibility narrowed | Limited to lawful permanent residents, Cuban-Haitian entrants, and COFA migrants; refugees and asylees removed | **2025-11-01** |
| SNAP-Ed eliminated | Nutrition education funding ends | FY 2026 |
| States' share of admin costs | Rises from about **50% to 75%** | **FY 2027, which begins 2026-10-01** |
| States' share of benefit costs | **5–15%** of benefits for states with payment error rates above 6%; none below 6% | FY 2028, with delays possible for high-error states |

**What this means for the probe, stated carefully:** several groups have lost or
may lose SNAP, and states face new costs starting within weeks. Food banks and
analysts expect pantry demand to rise, but that is a **prediction, not yet
evidence**. The probe's provider conversations should ask, *after* the
unprompted questions, whether each provider has seen changes since these rules
took effect. Their records are what would turn this into evidence.

**Not yet verified:** reports that SNAP participation has already fallen faster
than the Congressional Budget Office estimated. The source could not be
retrieved on the date above, so no figure is used here.

### The national survey has ended

USDA announced on 2025-09-20 that it was ending the annual Household Food
Security Report. The 2024 report (ERR-358, released 2025-12-30) is the last one.

**Consequence:** there will be no official national figure for 2025 onward.
Feeding America's modelled estimates and providers' own records become the main
sources. That makes the provider-data half of this probe *more* important, not
less: local operational records may soon be the best evidence that exists.

---

## What this data does *not* establish

- **What the gap is in any community.** Only local listening and provider records
  can show that.
- **That a new organization is needed.** Nothing here speaks to NEED §10.
- **Anything about individuals.** All figures are aggregate. No personal data was
  collected.
